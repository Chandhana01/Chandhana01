Final Reflection — Robinson Honors Fellowship

I first heard about the Antikythera Mechanism in a class at UNC, and two questions immediately bothered me. First, how have we not chemically dated the mechanism while confidently describing it as 2,000 years old? I understand a lot of archaeology is based on context and not direct testing. But for an artifact this rare, the difference between 1,000 and 2,000 years would radically change its significance. Second, how did we manage to invent such a complex object and then lose the skill for over a millennium? The gap in technology is not explained well. It's like having a chariot and then a starship. The mechanism survives textually in basically one literary account, from Cicero, and no object of similar complexity shows up again for about a thousand years. I fell deeper into this rabbit hole courtesy of Dr. Arbuckle and UNC Honors, and the Robinson Fellowship let me chase it properly.

The fellowship actually started in California. The mechanism has no living witnesses, so I ended up treating it like a cold case, and the first people I interviewed were not classicists at all. I talked to a former professional diver about what 1901 diving equipment could actually reach, which changed my assumptions about the search radius and what might still be on the seafloor. Over two-thirds of the mechanism has never been found, and a recent dive confirmed a similar shipwreck nearby. Could it be a sister ship? I spent two days with a private investigator who helped me map my research questions the way you would map a case, and I watched a woodworker demo a dovetail joint, because the mechanism's B1 gear seems to have one, visible on the CT scans. Dovetails were common on wood, but I've never seen a dovetail joint on bronze before, and neither had he. None of these people would have been findable for me from Chapel Hill; being in the middle of the San Francisco building scene meant someone could always introduce me to the next person, and every one of them read the object differently than the papers did. I also sat in a mechanical design studio talking through how I would even start to CAD a machine we only know from corroded fragments.

Then Egypt, which I went to as a counterexample. The Valley of the Kings had entire chapters of the Book of the Dead etched onto walls, encoding knowledge in a pretty much indestructible form over multiple millennia. Not to mention the preservation of the mummies themselves. It was surreal seeing a 3,500-year-old mummy just physically present in front of me, and it definitely messed with my sense of time scale. At Karnak I found what I believe was the Egyptian calendar in hieroglyphs, the same calendar that appears on the mechanism's front dial, plus dovetail-style joints in Egyptian stonework. I also visited a family-run stone workers' studio in Luxor and watched techniques that survived because many hands practiced them, in public, for generations. Egypt carved a huge amount of its knowledge into stone, where losing it would take real effort. Nothing like that happened with the mechanism; as far as anyone can tell, whatever went into making it stayed inside a workshop, or a few workshops, and inside the people who worked there. It got me thinking about how the same problem looks in our own time, and what it would take to make sure we don't lose critical information in the medium and long term.

In Alexandria I physically located the texts themselves: Cicero's De Natura Deorum, the Tusculan Disputations, the passage in De Republica, and the later mention in Claudian. Cicero describes these devices like they're known objects, implying they existed in plural, and the plural is the part that gets me, because it means there were more of them once. We have this one because a ship sank. It is also a very short list of texts; those few passages are close to the entire literary record of devices like this one.

Athens was the longest leg. I spent a full day with the mechanism and the shipwreck collection at the National Archaeological Museum. There are 82 fragments on display; I counted. It sits in a room with other, mostly unrelated objects, and a lot of visitors around me seemed disappointed by that, but the pull is real anyway: if ten visitors come to the museum, eleven come to see the mechanism. I met with the museum's metal conservators to discuss bronze composition, evidence for casting versus hammering, and what two thousand years of corrosion actually does, destroying surface detail while in places fixing structures firmly enough that they can still be read. Another museum contact would only speak with me unattributed, because formal statements require ministry paperwork, and even that told me something about how the object is managed. I had arrived asking why nobody has just analyzed the thing, and the answer came out in pieces over the week. Only very few people get a license to study the object. Usually only one study per topic is allowed, which leaves little room for cross-verification. A lot of the field's work is built on the same data that one team collected in 2005; at the cost of outing myself: those scans are as old as I am. And the fragments themselves are too fragile to move casually, which rules out most labs before the conversation even starts.

The elemental composition is where an obstacle turned into my favorite finding. We have only one published elemental analysis of the object, from destructive testing of tiny fragments in 1959, which found bronze with roughly 5% tin. A later test reportedly claimed some fragments were 60% tin, which would make it an entirely different object, but it was only ever stated at a live presentation, never published, and its authors can no longer be asked. Halfway through the trip I wrote my findings up and sent them to the researchers and mentors following the project. I got back a generous, detailed reply from one of the central figures in the field, confirming the story and the dead ends: lead-isotope analysis is essentially destructive and probably useless anyway given how mixed the Mediterranean metal trade was, and a synchrotron would be ideal but has no current prospect of happening. So I taught myself the one non-destructive route that had ever been floated, K-edge radiography, straight from the original methods papers, and that is where I found the gap. The technique detects an element by bracketing the jump in X-ray absorption at its K-edge. Copper's K-edge sits near 9 keV, too soft to survive the filters and penetrate anything, so tin (Z=50) falls inside the testable range but copper (Z=29) does not. Bronze is an alloy of copper and tin, which means the one proposed non-destructive method physically cannot see the dominant element of the alloy. I still can't answer what the mechanism is made of. The question I ended up with instead is whether you can tell surface tin from bulk tin without cutting anything, because K-edge radiography could still map where the tin sits, and a tin map would settle whether those 60% readings were surface plating or real.

My answer to the bigger question changed too. Originally I thought maybe we missed an entire industrial revolution by losing knowledge like this. (I still don't think "industrial revolution" is the right phrase, but it's the only one I've found that captures an entire era of technology that could have been built on these skills.) I now think the loss was quieter than that, and honestly the quieter version scares me more. The astronomical knowledge was never actually lost; it kept developing straight through Ptolemy and after. What died was the craft, a very niche thing, a few workshops with the knowledge to make these, and not much demand either. Nothing dramatic has to happen for a craft like that to disappear; it is enough that the few people who practiced it stopped, or died without training anyone. You can see a slow version of this in the modern field, too. The mechanism has been studied for over a century, long enough that many of the people who worked on it are retired or have passed away, some with data unpublished, and some details now exist only in other people's memories of a single presentation.

Some of what the trip did to me is more ordinary, and I don't want to skip it just because it's ordinary. I planned and ran weeks of travel through Egypt and Greece largely on my own as a young woman, arranging guides, drivers, hotels, and museum meetings as I went. Almost every door that opened for me opened because of a person. Cold emails helped some, but mostly it was showing up and talking with conservators and guards until someone decided to help me; my credentials, such as they are, never came up. I also got a ground-level education in sitting with uncertainty. In Athens, within a single week, I heard a guide claim the mechanism was extraterrestrial and a visiting professor doubt it was 2,000 years old at all; one person even questioned whether it was found in the sea in the first place. I could not verify or refute most of what I was told, so a lot of it, the contested tin numbers, the storeroom rumors, is sitting in my notes marked, in effect, unverified until I can chase each piece down.

The fellowship ended back in California, where I gave a talk on all of this at a private conference. I was nervous about it, because to my own ears my hypotheses sounded like conspiracy theory territory, an undergrad claiming there's a gap in a famous, century-old field. Nobody there treated it that way. People came at it from angles I hadn't planned for, some historical, some about what we are doing to preserve knowledge for the future, and one long conversation about how you encode knowledge that feels vague but persistent. Some attendees were adjacent to the Vesuvius Challenge, which recovered text from "unreadable" ancient scrolls with modern imaging, and to them an imaging gap was simply a problem statement worth working on. Afterward I went back to the wood shop and started a plywood prototype of the gearing, mostly to find out whether I understand the gear train well enough to actually build it.

Going forward: I want to push, in whatever ways an undergraduate can, for new multi-energy imaging of the mechanism. I want to finish a reconstruction from the CT data without inheriting priors from the existing models. And I want to write about the fragility of knowledge for a general audience, because it would be a stupid, stupid way to hinder our current and future progress, to lose things like this again. I keep thinking about the building scene in San Francisco and the making scene in Boston, places where knowledge survives because it lives in many hands at once. How do you capture what a scene makes possible for someone standing outside it? I don't know yet. It's on the list. For now the surface-tin question is where I plan to start, since of everything here it is the one piece somebody could actually settle.
