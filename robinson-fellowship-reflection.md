An Antikythera Field Trip
Robinson Honors Fellowship
University of North Carolina

I came across the Antikythera mechanism by pure chance, in a class I took with Dr. Benjamin Arbuckle as a required gen-ed for my degree. We were assigned to write a paper about any great archaeological wonder of the world, and I began my initial research by talking to ChatGPT about things I found interesting. It gave me a long list, as it usually does, and out of that list the Antikythera mechanism immediately stood out to me, even with absolutely no history knowledge besides what I was learning in the class.

I had taken a couple of astronomy classes at UNC, followed by a mechanical engineering internship and an analytical chemistry project. All of that put together, I knew how hard it is to make observations by just looking at the sky, then figure out the mathematics, and then design and encode that into a physical item that actually works. I knew I would have difficulty doing that even with modern CAD, let alone with no access to technology or tools and hardly even a complete discipline of mathematics. A single paper quickly became something I would spend nights and weekends exploring, purely because it was absolutely incredible that something like this even existed.

Two questions from that semester never left me. I remember asking Dr. Arbuckle over and over: but how have we not tested this object? Without doing so, how could we ever know for sure that it is 2,000 years old? I understood that archaeology is based on context, but for an artifact this rare, a difference between 2,000 and 1,500 years would radically change its significance. The second question was how we managed to invent such a complex object and then lose the skill for over a millennium. The gap in technology is not well explained. It's like having a chariot and then a starship. The mechanism also survives textually in basically one literary account, by Cicero.

This got me concerned about preserving the knowledge we currently have for future generations. How much of R&D in the world is based on recreating things somebody else already figured out? The semester ended, and I got out of it a list of questions, which turned into a Robinson Fellowship application, which turned into asking those questions more seriously and trying to answer them slowly. As someone with no archaeological background except that one gen-ed, the project first showed me that you don't have to have a certain background; you develop it as you pursue the inquiry. It also taught me that all initial hypotheses come from a place of not knowing, and that a mark of good research is having questions that seem a little bit like conspiracy theories, because good hypotheses sit outside the distribution of the norm before they are proven or disproven.

The fellowship interestingly started in California, as opposed to London as I had previously planned, and that change itself taught me a lot about how much can be learned from people in adjacent fields peering into a question, as opposed to working only with experts. The mechanism has no living witnesses, and many of the people who did the primary analyses are unfortunately deceased, so learning more about it started as a cold case investigation rather than a pure archaeological study. I got to talk to woodworkers, private investigators, mechanical engineers, and a former professional underwater diver, and their backgrounds helped me see the mystery from many, many different points of view as opposed to the singular one a pure researcher could have taken. The diver gave me an understanding of what 1901 diving equipment could actually reach, which changed my assumptions about the search radius and what might still be on the seafloor; two-thirds of the mechanism has never been found, and a recent dive confirmed a similar shipwreck nearby, so perhaps there is a sister ship. I also got to notice things that are normal enough to people in the space that no paper points them out, but were very novel to me, like the dovetail joint on bronze on the B1 gear, visible in the CT scans. The woodworker I met helped me think through the difference between a scanning artifact and a real joint; dovetails were common on wood, but neither of us had ever seen one on bronze. Being in the middle of the San Francisco building scene meant someone could always introduce me to the next person, and every one of them read the object differently than the papers did. I suspect this is also how the mechanism itself ever came into being. What it is, is a combination of the Egyptian calendar, Babylonian astronomy, Greek calendars, and the Panhellenic games including the Olympics, all put together into one machine, and in a thematically similar way my summer was about combining knowledge from multiple cities, people, and perspectives into one coherent understanding of a single artifact.

Egypt needs its own section. It gave me an entirely new sense of scale, something I didn't even know was possible. Looking at mummies that were three, four, five thousand years old, seeing the toenails of someone who existed multiple thousands of years before me, really shook me out of my sense of time. The Valley of the Kings had entire chapters of the Book of the Dead etched onto walls, knowledge encoded in a pretty much indestructible form, and at Karnak I found what I believe was the Egyptian calendar in hieroglyphs, the same calendar that appears on the mechanism's front dial, plus dovetail-style joints in the stonework. I also visited a family-run stone workers' studio in Luxor and watched techniques that survived because many hands practiced them, in public, for generations. Egypt carved a huge amount of its knowledge into stone, where losing it would take real effort. Nothing like that happened with the mechanism; whatever went into making it stayed inside a workshop, or a few workshops, and inside the people who worked there. The rest of Egypt made me realize that past civilizations were a lot smarter than we give them credit for as laypeople. We are more technologically advanced today, but that is because we stand on the shoulders of those who came before us, not because we as individuals are smarter than someone from 2,000 years ago.

In Alexandria I physically located the texts themselves: Cicero's De Natura Deorum, the Tusculan Disputations, the passage in De Republica, and the later mention in Claudian. That short list is close to the entire literary record of devices like this one, and Cicero describes them like known objects, implying they existed in plural. There were more of them once; we have this one because a ship sank. Alexandria, and later Athens, also made me realize that the inventors, philosophers, mathematicians, and astronomers we look back at never considered themselves with those labels. To them physics, philosophy, mathematics, astronomy, and building things were all one and the same, and that is something we have lost in the fragmentation into fields and sub-fields. Some of the best thinkers and builders of the past were precisely generalists.

Athens was the longest leg, and the biggest culture shock of the project. I spent a full day with the mechanism and the shipwreck collection at the National Archaeological Museum. There are 82 fragments on display; I counted. And if ten visitors come to the museum, eleven come to see the mechanism. When I first went up to one of the lead archaeologists at the museum, she was not happy to see me, which was very different from my experiences in America, where researchers are usually happy to indulge a student who is curious about the object. Another metal conservator was willing to talk to me, and quite enthusiastically, all while the first archaeologist glared at her for indulging my questions. We discussed bronze composition, evidence for casting versus hammering, and what two thousand years of corrosion actually does. An art historian later walked me through why the field is like this: archaeologists work extremely hard for very long years before any kind of promotion, so they usually don't appreciate open-sourcing knowledge to someone outside the institution. It gave me a lot more appreciation for how open American research culture is to newcomers. And the answer to my original question, why has nobody just analyzed the thing, came out in pieces over the week. Very few people get a license to study the object. Usually only one study per topic is allowed, which leaves little room for cross-verification. The fragments are too fragile to move casually. And a lot of the field is built on the same data one team collected in 2005; at the cost of outing myself, those scans are as old as I am.

Then there is the question of what the mechanism is actually made of, which is where my personal biggest obstacle lived. Coming from a scientific background, it was and continues to be out of the norm for me to keep inquiring about something I cannot test reasonably soon, and cannot ask the original testers about either. We have exactly one published elemental analysis of the object, from destructive testing of tiny fragments in 1959, which found bronze with roughly 5% tin. A later test reportedly claimed some fragments were 60% tin, which would make it an entirely different object, but that was only ever stated at a live presentation, never published, and its authors can no longer be asked. Halfway through the trip I wrote my findings up and sent them to the researchers and mentors following the project, and I got back a generous, detailed reply from one of the central figures in the field confirming the dead ends: lead-isotope analysis is essentially destructive and probably useless anyway given how mixed the Mediterranean metal trade was, and a synchrotron would be ideal but has no current prospect of happening. So I taught myself the one non-destructive route that had ever been floated, K-edge radiography, straight from the original methods papers, and that is where I found a gap. The technique detects an element by bracketing the jump in X-ray absorption at its K-edge. Copper's K-edge sits near 9 keV, too soft to survive the filters and penetrate anything, so tin (Z=50) falls inside the testable range but copper (Z=29) does not, which means the one proposed non-destructive method physically cannot see the dominant element of a copper-tin alloy. I still can't answer what the mechanism is made of. But K-edge radiography could still map where the tin sits, and a tin map would settle whether those 60% readings were surface plating or real, so my question shrank into one that somebody could actually answer.

My answer to the bigger question changed too. Originally I thought maybe we missed an entire industrial revolution by losing knowledge like this. Now I think what happened was smaller, and honestly it bothers me more. The astronomy itself kept developing straight through Ptolemy and after; what disappeared was the craft, a very niche skill held by a few workshops, with not much demand either. Nothing dramatic has to happen for a craft like that to disappear; it is enough that the few people who practiced it stopped, or died without training anyone. And you can see a slow version of this in the modern field too. The mechanism has been studied for over a century, long enough that many of the people who worked on it are retired or deceased, some with data unpublished, and some details now exist only in other people's memories of a single presentation.

Some of what the trip did to me is more ordinary. I planned and ran weeks of travel through Egypt and Greece largely on my own as a young woman, arranging guides, drivers, hotels, and museum meetings as I went, and most of the access I got came down to individual people deciding to help me, as opposed to any credential; my credentials, such as they are, never came up. I also had to learn to sit with things I could not verify. In a single week in Athens I heard a guide claim the mechanism was extraterrestrial, a visiting professor doubt it was 2,000 years old at all, and one person question whether it was even found in the sea. A lot of that, along with the contested tin numbers, is sitting in my notes marked, in effect, unverified until I can chase each piece down.

The fellowship ended back in California, where I gave a forty-minute talk on all of this at a private conference. The conspiracy theory feeling came forward hardest in the nerves before that talk: an undergrad claiming there's a gap in a famous, century-old field. What I found instead was that everyone really appreciated a very good series of questions, as opposed to a pretense of having answers just for the sake of having answers. Word of the talk got around enough that people started approaching me during meals, asking if I was the person who gave the archaeology talk, and several asked whether I might need more money for the research and told me to write to them if so. Some attendees were adjacent to the Vesuvius Challenge, which recovered text from "unreadable" ancient scrolls with modern imaging, and to them my imaging gap was simply a problem statement worth working on. Afterward I went back to the wood shop and started a plywood prototype of the gearing, mostly to find out whether I understand the gear train well enough to actually build it. And the resourcefulness this project demanded showed me that all it takes is for someone to think seriously about a problem for two months, asking a lot of questions, to arrive at questions no one has ever answered. The fact that sixty days can put you in the top percentile of people ever thinking about a problem makes me believe that so much more is possible.

Looking forward, this project has many more questions than I can realistically keep, so I am scoping it down to the pieces with a pathway forward: a timeline of the sub-mechanisms, and a proof of concept for non-destructively making an elemental map of the object, starting with the surface-tin-versus-bulk-tin question, since of everything here it is the one piece somebody could actually settle. It is a long shot to get permission from the museum, and it would require partnerships with other researchers, but I think it is a rabbit hole worth taking seriously to see where the path leads. I also want to finish a reconstruction from the CT data without inheriting priors from the existing models, and to write about the fragility of knowledge for a general audience, because it would be a stupid, stupid way to hinder our current and future progress, to lose things like this again. The project has gotten serious enough that I have decided to take some online community college classes and stay in California to work on this longer before transferring back to UNC, to see if there actually is something here that I can personally steward, and help us understand a little more about this object.
