The Rivet after Antikythera

A rivet is a pin given a second head after insertion — the tail peened or clenched until nothing can pull back through. By the Mechanism's lifetime it was already ancient (copper rivets hold dagger blades to hilts from c. 3000 BCE), but the Antikythera Mechanism marks something new: riveting as precision mechanical assembly, its disks and gears riveted together over square arbors. This timeline follows the rivet through the Mechanism's own world and the centuries just after it — Rome's armouries and bronze vessels, the first surviving rivet-built hull, the Byzantine geared sundial-calendar that continues the Mechanism's construction school, and the ghost ship at Sutton Hoo, recovered from its rivet rows alone.

The evidence, entry by entry

1 · Physical survivals

Riveted mail armour

Mail — interlocked iron rings, each typically closed by its own minute rivet — appears among the Celts around the 3rd century BCE and is adopted by Rome as the lorica hamata. It is the riveting technology of the Mechanism's own century, and remains standard armour into the 15th.

2 · CT evidence

Antikythera Mechanism — riveting as precision assembly

The AMRP tomographies show disks and gears riveted together, with square central holes seating on squared arbor ends; plates and fittings are fixed with pins, rivets, and burred-over shaft ends, with no threaded fasteners anywhere. The earliest surviving use of riveting not as gross fastening but as precision mechanical assembly — item 14 of your fabrication-methods list.

3 · Physical survivals (hoard)

Lorica segmentata's riveted fittings

Rome's segmental plate armour — in service from the Augustan period to the 3rd century CE — is a system of iron plates whose brass hinges, buckles, tie-loops and strap fittings are attached with copper-alloy rivets. The Corbridge hoard (found 1964) preserves dismantled armour parts and is the type's key physical evidence.

4 · Physical survivals

Riveted patches on Roman bronze vessels

Roman bronze vessels were maintained in use for generations: cracks and holes were covered with fitted bronze patches fastened by rivets, and handles, feet and mounts were riveted on. Experimental and archaeological studies of ancient bronze repairs document the practice across the empire — everyday riveting in the same craft world that produced the Mechanism.

  • EXARC: ancient repairs on bronze objects

  • Image: Roman bronze vessel, Wellcome Collection (Wikimedia Commons)

5 · Physical survival (bog find)

Nydam boat — the first surviving riveted hull

The oak boat from the Nydam bog, Denmark (c. 310–320 CE, now at Gottorf Castle) is the earliest surviving clinker-built hull: overlapping planks joined by iron nails clenched over diamond-shaped roves — a rivet formed in place. It is the technique's oldest complete witness, three centuries after the Mechanism.

  • Viking Ship Museum: rivets and roves
  • Image: The Nydam boat (Wikimedia Commons)

6 · Physical survival + published study

Byzantine sundial-calendar

The London Byzantine sundial-calendar (Science Museum 1983-1393, c. 400–600 CE) is the second-oldest geared mechanism known after the Antikythera Mechanism: a brass dial plate, suspension arm, Moon disc with gear, and arbors carrying four wheels and a ratchet, all in low-zinc brass of uniform composition (Field & Wright 1985). It is the Mechanism's disc-and-gear construction school, surviving into Byzantium. Note: its precise fastening details are not spelled out in the accessible summaries — see reading notes.

  • Field & Wright 1985, Gears from the Byzantines (Annals of Science)
  • Science Museum Group: the object, with photographs
  • Science Museum Group: Wright's reconstruction of the gearing
  • M. T. Wright, A Byzantine Sundial-Calendar (Florence 2009)

7 · Excavation record + survivals

Sutton Hoo's ghost ship & the Viking hulls

At Sutton Hoo (c. 625 CE) the ship's timber had dissolved entirely in the acid sand: the 27-metre hull was recovered as a ghost, traced plank by plank from its rows of iron rivets. The Oseberg and Gokstad ships (9th century) survive intact, riveted with nail-and-rove through every overlapping strake.

Reading notes & caveats

What counts as a rivet. A rivet is distinguished from a nail by the deliberate second head: the tail is peened, burred, or clenched after insertion so the joint cannot withdraw. That is why the clinker nail-and-rove counts as riveting, and why the Mechanism's burred-over shaft ends belong to the same family.

Scope. Per your direction, this timeline stays close to the Mechanism's era. The deep prehistory (first copper rivets c. 3000 BCE at Alacahöyük; Bronze Age dagger rivets) and the industrial story (Fairbairn's riveting machine 1837, the Forth Bridge's 6.5 million rivets, riveted airframes) are deliberately out of frame.

Byzantine sundial-calendar. Included as the Mechanism's construction school surviving into Byzantium — brass disc-with-gear and arbor work, per Field & Wright 1985. Whether its Moon disc and gear are riveted together in the Antikythera manner is not stated in the sources accessible from this environment, so the card claims the tradition, not the fastener; Wright's papers would settle it.
