From Saqqara to the slide rest · evidence-first history

The Dovetail Joint in Metal

The dovetail is one of the oldest fastenings we can physically hold: flared, wedge-shaped tails that lock against withdrawal. It appears fully formed in Egyptian woodwork around 3000 BCE. What took far longer is the move into metal — first as a metal cramp shaped like a dovetail (Achaemenid and Greek masonry, 6th century BCE), then as metal repair clamps, and in the gunsmith's and machinist's era as the standard fitted metal-to-metal joint. The Antikythera Mechanism sits at the hinge of that story: dovetail fittings have been identified on its great drive wheel b1 (see entry 4) — if upheld, the earliest known metal-to-metal dovetails.

The evidence, entry by entry

1 · Physical survivals

First Dynasty wooden dovetails

Dovetailed boxes and coffins survive from First Dynasty tombs (c. 3100–2900 BCE) — the joint arrives already well developed. Queen Hetepheres' bed canopy (4th Dynasty, c. 2550 BCE) uses dovetails and barefaced dovetails in its frame; the pictured funerary bed reproduction is at the MFA Boston.

  • Woodworking techniques in ancient Egypt (IntechOpen)
  • Did the ancient Egyptians use dovetails? — survey of museum pieces
  • X-ray tomography of Un-Montu's coffin — joinery revealed by CT (open access)
  • Image: Hetepheres bed, MFA Boston (Wikimedia Commons)

2 · Physical survivals + sockets

Wooden cramps tying stone

Double-ended dovetail "cramps" of wood tied adjacent stone blocks; museum survivals include a Middle Kingdom builder's cramp and a New Kingdom cramp inscribed for Sety I. Dovetail sockets remain cut into temple blocks. Museum object pages carry the photographs.

  • Builder's cramp, Middle Kingdom (Met Museum 544307, with photo)
  • Wooden cramp of King Sety I (National Museums Liverpool, with photo)
  • UCL Digital Egypt: cramps in building foundations

3 · In-situ physical survivals

First dovetails made of metal

At Pasargadae (c. 546 BCE, Tomb of Cyrus pictured) and Persepolis (from c. 515 BCE), ashlar blocks were tied with swallowtail clamps of iron sealed in poured lead — some still in place, and robbed clamp sockets are visible across both sites. Archaic Greek masonry used the same swallowtail form in iron or bronze set in lead. The dovetail shape is now executed in metal, though it still grips stone.

  • Archaic Greek masonry techniques
  • Clamp systems on the Athens Acropolis (IMEKO)
  • Image: Tomb of Cyrus, Pasargadae (Wikimedia Commons)

4 · CT evidence + contested reading

Antikythera b1 — the dovetail question

Per your identification, following Tony Freeth, dovetail fittings exist on the great drive wheel b1 — which would make them the earliest metal-to-metal dovetails known. What I could verify in the open literature from this environment: Freeth et al. 2021 documents b1's four spokes with bearing holes, pierced lugs, and peripheral pillars with "shoulders and pierced ends" carrying plates; I could not locate the word "dovetail" in the accessible texts, so the card carries the claim with attribution. 5 · Physical survivals + chemistry

Roman lead double-dovetail clamps

Dolia (1,000-litre storage jars) at Ostia were repaired with lead-alloy clamps shaped as double dovetails; ICP-MS analysis found lead hardened with silver and tin. Bronze vessels were likewise patched. Metal dovetail-shaped clamps are now routine repair hardware.

  • Cheung & Carpentieri, Life histories of the dolia of Ostia
  • Photograph: dolium rim repaired with lead double-dovetail clamp
  • EXARC: ancient repairs on bronze objects
  • Image: Dolia at Ostia Antica (Wikimedia Commons)

6 · Surviving arms + craft literature

Dovetails filed into iron barrels

On 18th-century longrifles, front sights and the under-lugs that pin barrel to stock sit in transverse dovetails sawn and filed into the octagonal iron barrel — a true metal-to-metal fitted dovetail, done by hand, surviving on thousands of period arms and still standard practice.

  • History of rifle sights (African Hunting Gazette)
  • Dovetail rail — engineering overview
  • Image: American longrifle, Met open access (Wikimedia Commons)

7 · Documented machines

Dovetail slideways in machine tools

Zakharov's special-purpose gun-barrel lathes at Tula (1812–25) ran their carriages on dovetail ways; by the 1830s–40s most British makers followed Whitworth in adopting dovetailed slideways. The dovetail becomes a precision sliding metal joint — the form still on milling machines and lathe cross-slides.

Reading notes & caveats

Three grades of "dovetail in metal." The timeline distinguishes (a) the dovetail shape executed in metal but gripping another material — Achaemenid/Greek cramps in stone, Roman clamps on ceramic; (b) metal patches on metal objects (Roman bronze repair, usually riveted rather than dovetailed); and (c) the fitted metal-to-metal dovetail — routine only from the gunsmith's and machinist's era, unless the Antikythera b1 dovetails are upheld, which would push (c) back to the 2nd–1st century BCE.

Antikythera b1. The b1 dovetail identification is carried here on your instruction, attributed to Tony Freeth. I could not locate the passage in the open-access Freeth texts reachable from this environment (which document spokes with bearing holes, pierced lugs, and shouldered, pierced pillars on b1) — supply the exact citation and it will be wired into entry 4 as primary evidence.

Dates. Egyptian dynastic dates are conventional approximations; "c. 1700s" for barrel dovetails marks the period when the practice is abundantly attested on surviving longrifles, not its first occurrence — transverse sight dovetails may go back further into the 16th–17th centuries, but I found no securely dated earlier example.
