# Notes on Egypt

I traveled through Egypt starting in Luxor, then flying to Cairo, then (after an unrelated week in Athens) ending in Alexandria, before flying out of Cairo back to Mumbai. This was more of a research trip than a tourist trip, so most of the books I read beforehand were about specifics of Egypt — particularly the New Kingdom and Ptolemaic Egypt — rather than the general history of Egypt. I think I still got a very interesting outlook on most of the sites I visited, but take everything here as the notes of an interested amateur, not an Egyptologist.

I say this reasonably often about most countries I visit, regardless of what continent they're on, but: Egypt definitely felt like India. Not India in 2026 — more the India I knew growing up in 2008, and probably a lot of the agricultural India you can still see today.

My main source beyond the trip itself was *Cleopatra* by Stacy Schiff, which I listened to as an audiobook while traveling and strongly recommend — more on why in the Alexandria section.

## Geography

Let's start with a map of Egypt. Interestingly enough, I didn't realize that Egypt is also inclusive of a region past the Suez Canal called Sinai. Sinai hardly has much civilization except for the coast of Sharm el-Sheikh, and it's more or less restricted for travel, at least if you're a tourist on foot or in a car.

The geography of the Nile is interesting because it flows from south to north, and because of that, Lower Egypt is actually the region around Cairo, closer to the Mediterranean, while Upper Egypt — where the Nile begins flowing — is, counter-intuitively, technically the south of Egypt.

When you begin the initial descent into Egypt by plane, you can see that most of the country is entirely deserted — very arid, very sand-filled and sand-colored — and then there's an almost straight line of stark contrast where the green crops grow against the plateaus near the Nile. If you can't watch this through a plane window, the second-best option is to look at it on Google Maps. It makes sense on paper, but it was genuinely interesting to see in person: venture out into the desert and you would definitely not survive, while right next to it is the lush greenness along the Nile. I can only imagine the kind of wealth this plain's fertility would have brought in historically.

And on the note of fertility: Egypt was historically considered very fertile, both for crops and for women. I think it's said that women would have twins and triplets at a rate that was not particularly common back then — though I don't actually know how old "back then" was.

One more thing about the Nile: it's a lot narrower than you would think. This tracks with a theory that the Nile used to be a lot wider, which meant it ran a lot closer to the Giza plateau than it does today — which matters for how the pyramids were built, and I'll get to that.

## The Kingdoms, or: Ancient Egyptians Thought the More Ancient Egyptians Were Stupid

For context, the rough sequence: the pyramids were built in the Old Kingdom, the Valley of the Kings was dug in the New Kingdom, and the Ptolemies and Cleopatra came at the very end, after Alexander the Great took over Egypt — or I guess after the Egyptians, quite gladly, changed course from being under Persian rule to Ptolemaic rule.

The difference between the Old Kingdom and the New Kingdom that stood out to me was tomb-building philosophy, and it amounts to the ancient Egyptians concluding that the more ancient Egyptians were quite stupid. The Valley of the Kings is a complex of underground tombs that you can actually go into, with walls still intact and most of the hieroglyphics still intact, because the whole thing stayed hidden. This is very intentionally different from the pyramids, because the Valley-of-the-Kings people thought the pyramid people were idiots. If the whole point of preserving the bodies of the dead was so they could continue their afterlives undisturbed, how stupid is it to build a giant beacon that says "dig here for treasure" smack in the middle of a place people could find easily?

By design, the Valley of the Kings is everything the pyramids are not. The pyramids are built above ground, huge and unmissable. The Valley is a place where you can't see anything from outside until you dig down into the tombs — and it's a whole valley of them. That inversion was pretty on-theme for how a lot of the temples were built too.

Speaking of temples: the Karnak Temple is the biggest ancient temple complex in the world, completed over roughly 2,000 years by around 30 rulers. It's a very physical representation of the era when Egypt and its kingdoms were flourishing, such that building this extensive a temple was just the norm. It's obviously hard to tell whether the people at the time were opposed to it or not, but the fact that such a grand structure still exists so many years after their existence is pretty remarkable, I would say.

## How the Pyramids Were (Probably) Built

Back to the narrow Nile. If the river really was wider and closer to the Giza plateau back then, most of the stone used to build the pyramids could have been floated up until basically the last minute, when it needed to be pulled by people up a ramp to actually build the structure.

This seems very plausible to me because the ancient Egyptians weren't stupid. They obviously wouldn't build something far inland if they could just harness the power of a river to make the job easier. It seems very likely this is what happened, and it's also, I guess, the most common belief among archaeologists about how the pyramids were built. I don't think the pyramids are actually as big of a mystery as we collectively think they are — we just don't stay up to date with the latest understanding of archaeology.

Also: I did not realize the pyramids were originally white.

And you can go inside them! It's a lot smaller inside than I expected — I thought there'd be more rooms toward the upper parts of the pyramid. The passages are extremely steep, extremely hot, and require some crawling, and there's not much to see, really. But the fact that you can do this at all without the building collapsing on you is still pretty remarkable. Well — I suppose the question was never really the building collapsing, considering it's been stable for this many thousands of years. I still thought it was interesting that this counts as safe, given there are no exits and no way of getting out if something did happen.

## The Original Long-Termists

A lot of the original colors still persist in the tomb and temple paintings, which I want to attribute to extremely skilled chemistry from before chemistry existed. And there are so many hieroglyphics on the walls that it really makes me think the Egyptians were the original long-termists — they cared about the record of their civilization persisting for millennia ahead of them, in a way that I don't think modern society has really placed much emphasis on.

Which raises a question I couldn't stop thinking about: all of this intricate handwork — systems that required a lot of people to cooperate — was built without any sort of project management system. How were people trained? How were they managed to produce output that obviously required high levels of coordination? The pyramids are the obvious example, but also things like extremely detailed sarcophagi with hand-cut pieces pasted on one at a time. You obviously needed multiple people working on the same object, and the same goes for hieroglyphics.

Were there different "handwritings" in hieroglyphics that we — or at least I — don't understand yet? What if somebody made a mistake? Could you erase it? Would you plaster over it and start again? How do you practice before writing on the wall, given that paper was still very expensive? Stone was a lot cheaper, but you can't exactly replace a wall if you botch it. Would someone proofread your work? Are there ways to make grammatical errors that mean something entirely different? I suspect there are — a lot of hieroglyphs look reasonably similar to each other, so you could plausibly make a mistake and have it go unnoticed for three thousand years.

## Gold Was Silver

Gold was everywhere: on tombs, on sarcophagi, used to make boxes, chairs, and chariots. But the jewelry was actually made of silver. This makes me think that gold, to the ancient Egyptians, was really not what we think of as gold today. It was — and this is a bad example, but still — more like stainless steel. Or maybe not stainless steel; maybe an expensive oak or wood. A prestige building material, as opposed to the precious metal. The actual "gold" of the jewelry economy was silver. Gold was silver.

## Luxor

We started the trip in Luxor, in Upper Egypt (the south — see above).

**The airport.** The Luxor airport was very interesting. We were greeted by a bunch of people wearing suits asking if we would like our visas fast-tracked. Many countries have some version of this through Global Entry or similar, but I've never seen a direct cash-for-speed visa situation. We got to skip all the lines — definitely making some local Egyptian enemies in the process — but the visa process dropped from about an hour to ten minutes, if I recollect correctly.

**Tipping.** The airport is also where I learned that you tip everybody in Egypt, and that you don't tip a lot, because the USD-to-Egyptian-pound conversion is very much on the side of the American dollar, let's just say. But this meant tipping *everybody*. And when I say everybody, I mean everybody: you'd go to the bathroom and there would be someone taking tissues out of the dispenser and handing them to you, and you would tip them. The upside is that everyone is extremely helpful, at a cost in gratitude of perhaps a dollar. The downside is that this gets annoying quickly. Luckily for me, I went with male friends, and people don't particularly approach a young woman who isn't alone — which was something I was worried about, and thankfully it did not happen much. This probably could have been different if I were a solo female traveler, but it's honestly unclear.

**Practical tips, of which I have exactly two.** Tip number one: get a hotel driver. Tip number two: although the driver is probably good, and so is the car, it is very unreasonable to expect seatbelts unless you pay a premium for a car that has them. This didn't really come as a surprise to me, but it's something to keep in mind if you're very Western and require a seatbelt.

**Hotels and heat.** There is basically one street of main hotels in Luxor — I think I counted perhaps six, with a new one being built — so if you're staying in Luxor, you're likely staying in one of them. The city seemed very under-hoteled, which is a great business idea if you're particularly looking to start a hotel somewhere. We showed up in the summer, which turned out to be the nicer end of the deal: for the cost of extremely hot weather and some unasked-for tans, we got upgraded to a nicer suite, and our hotel price was cut in half, because the hotels were mostly empty. The heat, for what it's worth, was not too bad — maybe 40°C, give or take, mostly give. But it was dry heat, and as long as you cover your skin it's very, very manageable, as opposed to the discomfort caused by humidity, which we didn't really encounter in any of the three cities.

**Living inside the ruins.** One thing that was interesting about Luxor is the number of ancient buildings just dilapidating along the street as you drive. There are regions where you have to drive out to a site to see the tombs and the Valley of the Kings and the temples, but there's also a good amount of it just *in* the city — and some of it is still functional. At the Luxor Temple, there's a section where a mosque was built over a Coptic Christian church that was built over the original temple, and the mosque is functional right now; you can go pray there if you choose to. It felt like an interesting mashup between the two extremes I usually see: the Chinese approach of demolishing old temples to build skyscrapers, and the opposite approach of impeccable day-one preservation. Egypt just... integrated it. It wasn't out of distribution for the culture and lives of the people living there today.

**Food.** There are usually two menus at restaurants: the English tourist menu and the Arabic menu. If you don't read Arabic, you're getting the English menu with marked-up prices. In dollar terms this didn't really mean much, so I was comfortable paying extra despite knowing we were getting mildly scammed — but it's something to keep in mind if you're trying local cuisine. Luxor was particularly not great for food if you're even a little bit specific, which I was: my doctor warned me not to eat fresh fruit or vegetables because I'd barely taken the typhoid vaccine before the trip, which made finding food noticeably harder.

The best cuisine in Luxor, and I mean better than any other cuisine we had there, was Chinese.

**The Chinese tourism pipeline.** Which brings me to: we saw more Chinese tourists than any other demographic, to the point where some local markets had more Chinese tourists than local Egyptians. When we asked around, we found that the Chinese tourist volume is such that the food is good, the tour guides speak Chinese, and a lot of hotel staff were actively interested in learning Chinese. So I suppose if you want to learn Chinese, there's a Chinese immersion school in Egypt you could always attend.

**Street fauna and franchises.** There are a lot of street cats, as opposed to India, where there are a lot of street dogs. There are also KFCs and Starbucks — the colonization of American fast food chains has spread far and wide.

**What Egyptians look like.** There didn't seem to be a standard Egyptian look, which I think also means there isn't really a particular Egyptian beauty standard. This goes back to how Egypt was, in an odd way, probably the America of its time: a lot of immigration, a lot of emigration, a lot of trade, and therefore a big mix of what people looked like — to the point where people assumed I was Egyptian, and that both of the friends traveling with me were also Egyptian. None of us are Egyptian.

**Conservatism, in both directions.** The city was both a lot more and a lot less conservative than I expected. Wearing headscarves was very common among women, but others were wearing tight, mermaid-fitting clothes, which I thought was fascinating. I was encouraged to carry a headscarf throughout the trip in case I needed to enter a temple or a more local town — but it also turned out to be the best possible way to keep my face from getting fried in the sun. I wonder how much of the practice just originated from the Bedouin culture of desert travelers protecting their skin, and then evolved into something more religiously required. I think it's quite likely it started as simply the most efficient way to cover your skin during long-duration sun exposure.

As for the men: people wear three-piece suits for jobs that really do not require them. This happened a lot at the airport, especially inside, which kind of makes sense — but then you go outside and the airport parking ticketing person, standing in the extreme heat, is also wearing a three-piece suit. Or you enter the hotel, and the person at the desk coordinating the hotel's taxis is in a suit. In 40°C (104°F). Very, very fascinating.

**Generative AI, government edition.** There was a lot of generative AI imagery visible in Luxor, in *government-run buildings* of all places. I joke about this, but I think OpenAI has a moral obligation to make sure ChatGPT generates good images if the rest of the world is going to base some percentage of its physical graphics on the software.

**Tour guides.** A lot of educated Egyptologists seem to become tour guides as their primary career, and a lot of them spoke five or six languages — Russian, English, Chinese, Arabic, and maybe a couple more. I initially assumed getting a tour guide was just another way to get scammed, but it proved very, very useful, especially for noticing particular sculptures and inscriptions that would otherwise have meant nothing to us.

That said, there were places where it was just very helpful to have Claude say things. It was good enough, on just Fable 5, to tell us where a particular inscription was within a complex — specific enough to say "go to the south wall of the Karnak Temple, on the external-facing side" to find the exact thing I was looking for.

**The mummy.** It was very cool — and surreal — to see a mummy for the first time. It definitely fully fucked with my sense of timescale, because you're looking at a physical human who existed 3,000–4,000 years ago and had an entire life, and their toenails are intact. You can just viscerally feel that they used to exist. Your brain auto-corrects and assumes this person didn't die that long ago, and then you do the math and realize how many lifetimes before yours this person existed, and it's entirely surreal. A little creepy, but more surreal than creepy.

I also have no clue what physical remains of our society will still exist 3,500 years from now by default. Maybe a lot more garbage than we'd like. Maybe we get good at incineration. Or maybe we won't be on Earth at all (Mars, etc.).

**The fun guy.** There was also this fun statue — the single most fun statue of all the statues I found. He seems like a cool guy. He seems like he could be in a frat and work in financial services in New York if he wanted to. A bunch of these king statues look like the king was mewing in there. There are some in Luxor, and I think some in Cairo as well, but it was hilarious to learn that the GigaChad is not a Gen Z phenomenon at all. In fact, it is quite old.

## Cairo

From Luxor we took a plane to Cairo — about an hour and a half, if I recollect correctly — and Cairo was obviously very, very different from Luxor. I wish I'd spent more time in the city proper; most of my time was on the Giza plateau or driving through town on the way there.

**Heliopolis.** We got to see what looked like Hindu temple architecture in the middle of town, in a district called Heliopolis, and there's an interesting story about how that section of the city came to be — I think it was designed by an architect from Berlin, which is why the style of the buildings in this region is so different from everywhere else.

**The pyramids are right there.** The pictures don't really show this, but the pyramids are a lot closer to civilization than I was led to believe. You can see the pyramids on your regular highway drive.

**The cars.** A lot of the cars seemed very dilapidated — much more than in any other developing country I've been to. My theory is that the exchange rate between the Egyptian pound and the currencies of the countries that actually manufacture and sell cars is bad enough that you have very little incentive to buy a new car when you could repair the old one and keep it functional. The cars definitely look beaten up in every possible way — not just internally, with the lack of seatbelts, but also scratches, dents, and sheer age. The overall highway aesthetic is interestingly similar to something from the early 2010s, give or take.

**Museum party trick.** There was a fun stand at the museum that would write your name in hieroglyphics, which I thought was a very cool party trick.

**Ice cream.** On the note of my ice cream hobby, I of course had to spend some time finding interesting flavors. Cairo delivered: Lotus, Hibiscus, Mastic, and Cassata.

**Horses.** I got to ride horses in the Sahara Desert — or rather, *gallop* horses in the Sahara Desert. For context, I have walked a horse entirely on my own exactly once, and walked horses slightly faster with assistance as a little kid. It was hilariously stupid how quickly you're allowed to gallop a horse in Egypt, just because there are no prerequisites. Sure, it's probably a bit more unsafe than doing it in America with many, many requisites, but it was super fun and I would just highly recommend it.

**Camels.** Camels are fucking huge. I don't think people talk about this enough — or maybe I'm just not in the rooms where camel height is discussed in particular detail — but they're huge. I have never worked my abs as much as I did being on a horse galloping very fast through the Sahara, except possibly being on a camel that was also, for some reason, running. Definitely would highly recommend.

**Food, unexpectedly.** Cairo had an appreciable amount of Indian food, which I was not expecting — but then, neither was I expecting to see a Hindu-temple-styled building in the middle of the city.

## Alexandria

The last leg of the trip was Alexandria, and this is where the Stacy Schiff book comes in. I strongly recommend reading *Cleopatra* by Stacy Schiff — I listened to the audiobook over the course of the trip, and it turned out to be an eerily good companion, because the book follows the same route I did. It opens with a bit of introduction to older Egypt, then jumps to Cleopatra meeting Caesar — roughly the Cairo of my itinerary, by my understanding — then their relationship and his death, and then her relationship with Mark Antony takes her to Greece. I happened to listen to that section while I was actually in Athens, where Cleopatra and Mark Antony also were, according to the book. And I arrived in Alexandria just as Cleopatra and Mark Antony were spending the last days of their lives after being cornered by Octavian. The day I arrived in Alexandria, they both died in the book.

Some things from the book that were new to me: Cleopatra had children with both Julius Caesar and Mark Antony, and it was her daughter, Cleopatra Selene, who went on to outlive her sons. It would have been an incredibly different timeline had her son with Caesar survived — someone descended from the Greeks, the Romans, *and* the Egyptians at once. That would have been a pretty powerful position to be in. No wonder he "passed away."

There's also an urban legend that the C-section was named after Cleopatra's son with Caesar, Caesarion. The guides share this one too, but it's kind of obviously not true.

**The Library.** The Library of Alexandria was burned down multiple times, but on the same site there's a newer library you can visit, and it's very cool.

**Have a local.** In Alexandria we got to spend time with a local, and it was very helpful to have someone who spoke Arabic. If you're planning a trip, it might be worth trying to find a friend who lives in town, or bringing a friend who speaks Arabic.

**The city itself.** Alexandria felt a lot more modern — I'm sure Cairo is too, I just didn't spend time in those parts of town. It's very coastal, very beautiful, with warm beaches, very good seafood, and very good Egyptian food in general. I tried more Egyptian food in a couple of days in Alexandria than in all the other places put together.

**The missing ruins.** One thing that stood out: Luxor and Cairo have these remnants of past civilizations everywhere, in a way that Alexandria doesn't — even though Alexandria was an extremely flourishing city in exactly that period. There isn't much to *see*. Most of the value I got from Alexandria came from talking to people, checking out the library, and mostly just reading about the city while in the city.

It's a beautiful city, don't get me wrong — the most modern of the three, and the one I'd most likely live in if I had to. But this absence is also why it reminded me of American cities, particularly San Francisco: a place where history is being made, and those who understand that can feel it in the air unlike in any other city — while those who don't just see another city, more the hippie-era town it used to be than the place where the future is currently being written. It makes me a little sad that, as of now, there's not much anyone will be able to look back on in San Francisco that captures this particular era of flourishing. It also doesn't help that it's built directly on a fault line. But that's beside the point.

## The Flight Out

Leaving on the flight from Cairo back to India — specifically Cairo to Mumbai — I have never seen that many men on a plane, ever. There were multiple points, while boarding and on the aerobridge, where I would look in every direction I could see and count zero women. I eventually counted: I think there were six women on the entire plane, including the cabin crew — me, one little girl, and maybe three or four other adult women. Which left me very intrigued about what exactly is happening between Mumbai and Cairo.

They clearly didn't seem like tourists — and even if they were, a plane full of men traveling alone to tour Cairo doesn't seem right, especially since this wasn't the season anyone tours Egypt. I don't really have a theory. My friend suggests some sort of trade or labor agreement between the countries, but my understanding is that Egypt is pretty self-sufficient when it comes to labor, so I don't understand what this could have been. Open question.

## Random Stuff I Found Interesting

- The mummy I saw is older than Cleopatra, who is older than Jesus. Cleopatra is closer in time to us than to the people who built the pyramids.
- Luxor's best cuisine was Chinese. There are Chinese-speaking tour guides, Egyptians actively trying to learn the language, and markets with more Chinese tourists than Egyptians in them.
- The pyramids were originally white. Nobody seems to know this, or that you can go inside them.
- Luxor appears to be genuinely under-hoteled — roughly six hotels on one street and mostly full service of the entire tourist population. If you're looking to start a hotel somewhere, there's your market inefficiency.
- Going to Egypt off-season is a great trade: for the cost of unasked-for tans, your room gets upgraded to a suite and your hotel bill gets cut in half.
- People are very into wearing three-piece suits for jobs that do not need them — airport parking ticketing, hotel taxi coordination — in 40°C (104°F) heat.
- The friend I met in Alexandria works on brain-computer interfaces, and apparently a lot of money flows into Egyptian universities specifically to work on BCIs.
