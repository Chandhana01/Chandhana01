They want to build Ozempic for sleep.
Here’s what that actually means.

Capable is a lab that wants to make a drug that lets healthy people sleep less — 4–6 hours a night — while staying healthy, by copying rare humans who are naturally built that way. This page walks through the biology and the chemistry behind that plan, with diagrams you can play with. Every claim is tagged as either from the memo or established science, with sources.

Start here

The whole thing in 90 seconds

1 · The clue

A few rare families carry gene variants that let them feel fully rested on ~6 hours of sleep, with more energy and seemingly lower disease risk — not less. Nature already built the thing Capable wants to make.6–10

2 · The target

Those variants funnel into a few molecular “switches” — chiefly a gene called DEC2 and a brain signal called orexin — that control how much sleep your brain demands.1,6,7

3 · The drug

They build molecules — especially peptides (mini-proteins, the same drug class as Ozempic) — that nudge those switches, then test them in mice within a week.15–17

Why “Ozempic for sleep”? Ozempic is a peptide that flips one powerful hormonal switch (the GLP-1 system) and gets a big whole-body effect. The memo’s bet is that sleep has a similar master switch you can flip with a peptide — so you reclaim waking hours without the harm of normal sleep deprivation. from the memo
What they're proposing

“Induce a short-sleeper phenotype in adults”

In plain terms: take a trait that some people are born with (needing little sleep), and turn it into something you can give an adult as a medicine.

from the memo   The memo’s own framing: “use naturally occurring short-sleeper variants to develop a targeted therapy that safely decreases sleep need while preserving health… modulation of key sleep pathways such as DEC2 & orexin will allow us to pioneer safe approaches to reclaim thousands of waking hours.”

Two reasons they think this is more than a productivity hack

Brain protection.

Short-sleeper mice accumulate less Alzheimer’s-type damage (beta-amyloid and tau “plaques”), and short-sleeper families appear to have low rates of neurodegeneration. So the same switch might also slow brain disease.11–13

Real diseases first.

The first patients wouldn’t be biohackers — they’d be people with narcolepsy, idiopathic hypersomnia, and treatment-resistant depression, where excessive sleepiness or low energy is the disease.14–17 from the memo

⚠️ Reality check, not in the memo: this is an early-stage research plan. As of the memo there is no approved drug, no human trial — the positive data shown is a peptide changing how long mice stay inactive overnight (n=15 per group). Everything below explains the mechanism they’re betting on, not a proven treatment.
The clues from nature

The “short sleeper” genes — click each to open

These are real, published human variants. Capable wants to mimic their effect with a drug instead of editing your DNA. Tap a card.

▾ established

DEC2 (gene: BHLHE41)

The master dimmer switch

DEC2 is a transcriptional repressor — a protein whose job is to turn other genes down. One of the genes it sits on and silences is the gene for orexin, the brain’s “stay awake” signal.

The famous short-sleeper mutation (P384R) breaks DEC2’s grip on the orexin gene. With the brake off, the brain makes more orexin → more wakefulness → less sleep needed. Mice carrying it sleep less but stay healthy for life.1,6,7

Tellingly, an orexin blocker partly cancels the short-sleep effect — strong evidence that DEC2 works through orexin.1

▾ established

ADRB1 (β1-adrenergic receptor)

A wake-promoting receptor

This is the receptor that adrenaline-type signals use. A short-sleeper family carries the A187V variant, found in “wake-promoting” neurons in a brain-stem region called the dorsal pons.

The variant makes those wake neurons more excitable, so the brain spends more time awake and needs less sleep (~1 hour less in mice).9

A related ADRB1 finding: the mutant version improves REM sleep and reduces tau buildup in a mouse model of brain disease.12

▾ established

mGluR1 (gene: GRM1)

A glutamate receptor

mGluR1 reads glutamate, the brain’s main “go” signal. Two mutations in this receptor were found in natural short sleepers and shown to change sleep in mice.8

It’s a third, independent route to the same destination — which is the memo’s point: several different genes all converge on “need less sleep, stay healthy.”

established   The pattern that matters: DEC2, ADRB1, and mGluR1 are different molecules, but they all tilt the brain toward wakefulness without making people sick. That’s the green light Capable is acting on — if many natural “off-ramps” lead to safe short sleep, a drug aimed at one of them might too.

“How the drugs fit into the receptors”

Lock and key: docking a drug into a receptor

A receptor is a protein antenna on a cell. The orexin receptor OX2R is a GPCR — when the right molecule slots into its pocket, the receptor changes shape and fires a signal inside the cell. An agonist is a key that turns it ON. Press the button to dock one.

drug molecule (key) receptor pocket (lock) G-protein (inside messenger)
Press Dock the agonist. The drug slides down into the receptor’s pocket; the receptor changes shape and switches on the G-protein inside the cell, which fires the “stay awake” signal.
established Real example: TAK-861 (oveporexton) is an experimental OX2R-selective agonist — a small molecule that docks into OX2R and fires the same signal orexin would. It promotes wakefulness in mice and monkeys and improves narcolepsy-like symptoms.16 This is exactly the kind of “key” Capable’s sleep program is chasing.
from the memo Why agonist matters: the memo notes the first GLP-1 drugs hit one receptor; Eli Lilly’s tirzepatide wins by being an agonist at two; and that a team member made the first published tetra-agonist (one molecule, four receptors). More precise “keys” = better drugs.

Why peptides, why "Ozempic"

The Ozempic playbook they’re copying

What Ozempic does

GLP-1 is a natural gut hormone — a peptide. Ozempic (semaglutide) is a lab-built copy that docks into the GLP-1 receptor (also a GPCR) and flips a metabolic master-switch: less hunger, better blood sugar. One precise peptide, one big effect.— established

What Capable wants

Same strategy, different switch: a precise peptide that docks into a sleep pathway (orexin / OX2R, and upstream DEC2) and flips a master-switch for wakefulness — “reclaim thousands of waking hours.” from the memo

Bigger keys, then sharper keys. Tirzepatide hits two receptors (GIP + GLP-1) and beats single-target semaglutide on weight loss — proof that multi-target peptides can outperform. Capable’s team includes people who push exactly this frontier: dual- and tetra-agonist peptides, and “stitched/stapled” peptides that survive longer in the body. established + memo
"Explain how they would make it"

Building a peptide drug, one amino acid at a time

Peptides are short chains of amino acids (protein building blocks). Labs build them on a tiny plastic bead using solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) — the method Capable’s synthesizer runs in hours. It’s a repeating two-move cycle. Step through it:

step 0 / 6
Each colored block = one amino acid added to the chain.
  1. Anchor. Attach the first amino acid to the resin bead. Its reactive end wears a protective “cap” (an Fmoc group) so it can’t react yet.
  2. Deprotect. Wash with a base (piperidine) to pop the cap off — now the end is reactive and ready to bond.
  3. Couple. Add the next (still-capped) amino acid plus an activator. It forms a peptide bond, extending the chain by one link.
  4. Wash & repeat. Rinse, then loop steps 2–3 for every amino acid in the sequence. (Capable’s record-setting hardware does each link in seconds.)
  5. Cleave. Cut the finished chain off the bead (strong acid, TFA) and strip remaining protecting groups.
  6. Purify & test. Clean it up, confirm the exact molecule, then dose it into mice — sometimes the same week.
Press Next step to build the peptide. SPPS is just “deprotect, couple, repeat” until the chain is the length you designed.

Standard Fmoc solid-phase peptide synthesis (Merrifield method, Fmoc variant): iterative piperidine deprotection + activated coupling, then TFA cleavage.B1

The pro move: “stapling” the peptide so it survives

A loose peptide gets chewed up fast in the body. Capable’s team works on stapled / stitched peptides: you insert two special amino acids with reactive “arms,” then a catalyst (ring-closing metathesis) fuses the arms into a hydrocarbon staple. That staple locks the peptide into its active α-helix shape, making it dramatically more stable and better at getting into cells.B2

Two olefin-bearing amino acids + ring-closing metathesis → a hydrocarbon staple that stabilizes the α-helix. The memo highlights team members behind “stitched” GLP-1 peptides and stapling chemistry.B2
The plan

From mouse to medicine

The memo’s stated sequence. Hover/tap each stage.

First they prove a target changes sleep in mice, then build a mouse-ready drug (under a year, per the memo), optimize it and run safety studies, then the formal pre-human (“IND-enabling”) work, aiming first at severe narcolepsy, hypersomnia, and depression. from the memo
